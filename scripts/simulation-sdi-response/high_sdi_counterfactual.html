Summary

This report tests whether lower-SDI attention-burden alignment can be explained as a passive consequence of the process that produces observed High SDI alignment plus shared disease burden. The null expectation is that lower-SDI concentration-index trends should track the High-SDI-calibrated simulation ribbon. Under that interpretation, any apparent lower-SDI alignment would be dictated by the determinants of GWAS prioritisation in High SDI settings and by overlap in disease-burden rankings across SDI regions.

The main finding is that lower-SDI regions generally perform worse than expected under this transfer null in the time-varying attention analysis, for both the active and reversed concentration-index definitions. This suggests that observed time-varying GWAS attention is not representative of the broader set of allocations compatible with High SDI alignment. It is concentrated on a subset of High-SDI-compatible conditions that happen to be less compatible with lower-SDI burden than alternative High-SDI-calibrated allocations would be.

The all-time analysis gives a more nuanced result. When asking whether lower-SDI disease burden is concentrated among conditions that have received GWAS attention, all-time attention is closer to the null expectation. When asking whether GWAS attention is concentrated on lower-SDI high-burden conditions, lower-SDI regions again perform worse than expected. This suggests some accumulated coverage of lower-SDI-relevant conditions, but insufficient prioritisation intensity toward the conditions that matter most in lower-SDI settings.

Aim

This analysis tests whether lower-SDI attention-burden alignment is compatible with a High-SDI transfer null. Under this null, the process that produces observed High SDI alignment is treated as sufficient to explain lower-SDI alignment once shared burden structure is accounted for. This process may include High SDI disease burden, tractability, statistical power, cohort availability, historical research momentum, and international collaborations; the simulation does not distinguish those mechanisms.

The simulation is a calibrated permutation analysis. It preserves each GWAS attention window’s observed sparsity and attention-score inequality, breaks the observed mapping between conditions and attention scores, then keeps simulated attention allocations that reproduce the observed High SDI alignment metric. Those same kept allocations are evaluated against each SDI region’s burden ranking.

Methodology

Analysis universe

The main analysis uses sliding-window GWAS attention scores and SDI burden data for all ages, both sexes, and DALY counts. The condition universe is the same condition set used by the regional concentration-index analysis after zero padding attention windows. Conditions with zero GWAS attention remain in the analysis universe.

COVID-19 is excluded before constructing observed metrics or simulated allocations. The exclusion is used because a single pandemic-era condition can dominate the support of possible permutations, narrowing the counterfactual range in a way that is not useful for testing the broader GWAS condition canon. Because COVID-19 is not uniformly high-burden across SDI regions, this exclusion may be conservative for lower-SDI below-null findings in some years.

Metrics

The primary metric is the attention-burden concentration index:

CI_active = CI(GWAS attention ranked by DALY burden)

Positive values mean GWAS attention is concentrated among higher-burden conditions in the selected SDI region and year.

The reversed metric is calculated separately as a diagnostic:

CI_reversed = CI(DALY burden ranked by GWAS attention)

Positive values mean disease burden is concentrated among conditions with greater GWAS attention. Because this answers a different question, the active and reversed metrics are calibrated in separate simulation runs.

Candidate generation

For each year or attention window, the simulation starts with the observed vector of GWAS attention scores:

observed_scores = {0, 0, ..., nonzero attention scores}

Each candidate allocation preserves the exact observed nonzero score values and the exact number of zero-attention conditions. A candidate is generated by:

1. sample n_nonzero conditions from the condition universe
2. assign the observed nonzero scores to those selected conditions
3. assign zero attention to all other conditions

The candidate generator uses a proposal mixture so that enough High-SDI-aligned allocations are available for calibration:

proposal 1:
  randomly choose nonzero conditions
  randomly assign nonzero score magnitudes

proposal 2:
  randomly choose nonzero conditions
  assign larger scores to higher High SDI burden ranks, with rank noise

proposal 3:
  preferentially choose higher High SDI burden conditions
  assign larger scores to higher High SDI burden ranks, with rank noise

The rank-noise levels range from strongly High-SDI-shaped to weakly High-SDI-shaped. The weighted-selection proposals make higher High SDI burden conditions more likely to receive nonzero attention before score magnitudes are assigned. The proposal mixture is not itself the estimand; it is a practical sampler used to discover enough candidate allocations near the observed High SDI calibration target.

Calibration

For each year, the simulation generates 5^{4} candidate allocations and keeps the 5000 allocations closest to the observed High SDI metric. The active and reversed analyses use different calibration targets:

active run:
  keep allocations closest to observed High SDI CI_active

reversed run:
  keep allocations closest to observed High SDI CI_reversed

High SDI is therefore a calibration check, not an independent test. The lower SDI regions are the test regions: the High-SDI-calibrated allocations are projected onto each lower-SDI burden ranking to estimate the null distribution expected from shared burden structure alone.

Summaries and interpretation

For each SDI region and year, the kept allocations define a null distribution. The report summarizes:

sim_lci      = 2.5th percentile of simulated CI
sim_median   = median simulated CI
sim_uci      = 97.5th percentile of simulated CI
observed_ci  = empirical regional CI

If the observed lower-SDI CI lies inside the null ribbon, the observed regional alignment is compatible with the High-SDI transfer null. If it lies below the ribbon, observed alignment is worse than expected under a generic High-SDI-calibrated canon. If it lies above the ribbon, observed alignment is stronger than expected under the transfer null.

This is a sufficiency test, not a causal proof. Compatibility with the null means that the observed pattern can be reproduced by High SDI alignment plus shared burden structure. It does not prove that High SDI prioritisation caused the observed pattern.

Interpretation guide

The lower-SDI observed line is interpreted relative to the High-SDI-calibrated null ribbon:

observed inside null ribbon:
  lower-SDI alignment is compatible with passive High-SDI transfer

observed above null ribbon:
  lower-SDI alignment is stronger than expected
  possible evidence of condition prioritisation beyond passive High-SDI transfer

observed below null ribbon:
  lower-SDI alignment is weaker than expected
  High-SDI-shaped prioritisation is worse than a generic High-SDI-calibrated
  allocation for lower-SDI burden

The last case is especially informative. It means the empirical GWAS canon is not representative of the broader High-SDI-compatible allocation space. Among allocations that could satisfy the High SDI calibration target, observed GWAS attention is concentrated on a subset of conditions that are relatively less important in lower-SDI regions than other plausible High-SDI-aligned alternatives.

Calibration diagnostics

Calibration quality is reported for every year. Windows are flagged when:

median_abs_error > 0.02
or
max_abs_error > 0.05

A warning indicates that the kept allocations do not closely reproduce the High SDI calibration target and should not be used for strong inference without increasing the candidate count or tuning the proposal sampler. Warning windows remain visible in plots using a different point shape.

Sliding-window and all-time analyses

The active and reversed metrics are calibrated in separate simulation runs. The active ribbon keeps allocations closest to observed High SDI attention-burden concentration index; the reversed ribbon keeps allocations closest to observed High SDI reversed concentration index.

The all-time sensitivity uses the all-time attention score distribution and evaluates it against each burden year.

All-time attention sensitivity

This sensitivity repeats the counterfactual using all-time GWAS attention scores and each year of DALYs. It asks whether the accumulated GWAS condition canon is narrower than a generic High-SDI-calibrated canon across the burden history. Because the all-time attention distribution is dense and highly unequal, calibration warnings should be checked before placing strong weight on these ribbons under the default candidate count.

Interpretation of Results

The four analyses separate two related questions:

active CI:
  Is GWAS attention concentrated on high-burden conditions?

reversed CI:
  Is disease burden concentrated among conditions that receive GWAS attention?

time-varying attention:
  What do specific GWAS attention windows do?

all-time attention:
  What does the accumulated GWAS canon do?

For time-varying attention, lower-SDI regions tend to fall below the High-SDI-calibrated null for both active and reversed concentration-index definitions. This indicates that individual GWAS attention windows do not merely reflect passive transfer from High SDI burden priorities. They perform worse for lower-SDI burden than a generic High-SDI-calibrated attention allocation would. Substantively, this suggests that the observed changing GWAS canon is a particular slice of the High-SDI-compatible allocation space: it is concentrated on conditions that are less relevant to lower-SDI burden than alternative allocations that would still reproduce observed High SDI alignment.

For all-time attention, the reversed metric is closer to the null expectation. This suggests that the accumulated GWAS literature has some coverage of conditions carrying lower-SDI disease burden. In other words, lower-SDI burden is not entirely outside the studied condition universe once GWAS attention is pooled across time.

However, the all-time active metric again suggests weaker lower-SDI alignment than expected under the High-SDI transfer null. This means that even where lower-SDI-relevant conditions have received some GWAS attention, the amount of attention is not concentrated on the highest-burden lower-SDI conditions as much as the null would predict.

Taken together, the results suggest a distinction between coverage and prioritisation intensity. GWAS has accumulated some condition coverage relevant to lower-SDI disease burden, but the distribution of attention remains more skewed toward a narrow subset of High-SDI-compatible conditions than expected under passive High-SDI transfer. The evidence does not support concerted lower-SDI-responsive condition prioritisation, which would appear as lower-SDI alignment above the null ribbon. Instead, the stronger pattern is that observed lower-SDI alignment is often worse than expected even under a High-SDI-calibrated null. The compatibility-count tables above quantify this pattern by reporting how many years fall below, inside, or above the null ribbon for each SDI region.

This interpretation should be weighted by calibration quality. Windows flagged with calibration warnings are useful descriptively, but stronger claims should focus on well-calibrated windows or be revisited with a larger candidate count and tuned proposal sampler.
